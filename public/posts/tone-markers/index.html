This is the best article ever /hj

Tone indicators are definitely among my favourite features of written language used on the internet. During an in-person conversation, the tone of voice, facial expression and overall body language of your conversation partner would (ideally) give away if they’re joking, being sarcastic or something of similar nature. When you lead a conversation on the internet though, it usually takes place in written form – meaning these cues (which might already be difficult to spot for some) get completely lost. Thankfully – given that the writer is enlightened enough to use them – tone indicators come to the rescue and bluntly state (or indicate, if you may), well, the tone of the message. But… are they really as unambiguous as they like to pretend to be? (spoiler alert: no)

As jan Misali pointed out in their video, there are at least 3 distinct ways to interpret the /hj indicator. In my mind, that’s enough of a reason for it to be plunged into the depths of obscurity; in this article, I would like to map out as many possible meanings of this indicator as possible and assign a new indicator to each of the meanings. (As per this German saying: “Ordnung muss sein!”1)

Let me begin by describing the cases where I personally would use this godforsaken ambiguation. Ironically, cases where the meaning is an actual half-joke are only a small minority. Disclaimer: there is approximately a very high chance that I forgot something. Also, naturally I can’t think of any examples right now so I’ll just be adding them whenever I think of something.

  1. A text whose literal meaning isn’t true (or meant sincerely), but the point behind it, or the problem illustrated by it, is a sincere point or an actual problem. Its “subdivisions” could include:
    • an unrealistic scenario said to illustrate a real problem
      • example: “If I fail another exam, I’ll kill myself” – I will do everything I can to avoid failing another exam, but I won’t actually kill myself.
    • the author considers the “point behind the text” to be true, but it would be inappropriate for thew to say so without obfuscating
      • example: saying “I am the best pianist ever” after successfully playing a moderately difficult piece;
  2. A text whose literal meaning isn’t true (or meant sincerely), but which would be funny had it been said sincerely in the given context.
  3. A text whose literal meaning is true (or meant sincerely), but which was deliberately worded in a way that’s meant to be funny. Adjacently, a true statement where the fact alone that it’s true makes it funny.
    • example: “I have
  4. A text which is funny on its own, but given the context is actually pointing towards a serious point/problem.
  5. A text which doesn’t seem particularly funny on its own, but the context is what’s supposed to make it funny.
  6. A “Schrödinger’s joke” or “for legal reasons, a joke” – text where the author is unsure whether it actually is a joke or not when the message is being sent; this will be decided based on the reaction of the recipient(s) or other circumstances.

Originally, I was intending to name all usecases I use somewhat regularly and just number them (like jan Misali in their video). After some thought, I realised it isn’t ideal to have to look through a list of numbered tone indicators just to express what you mean – let alone for the recipients, getting messages ended with cryptic messages like “/hj45” with the only way of knowing what I meant being having to sift through the first 44 meanings. That said, I would also like to avoid a solution where you have to explain exactly what you mean using three full sentences put in parentheses behind the actual message.
